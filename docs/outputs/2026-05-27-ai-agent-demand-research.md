# AI Agent Development Demand Research

Date: 2026-05-27
Status: Research draft
Purpose: Re-ground the business model in real pain, real demand, and real buying logic before redesigning the landing page or monetization.

---

## Research question

Before deciding what to sell under `AI Agent Development`, answer:

1. What pain are businesses actually trying to remove?
2. Which pain is expensive enough to fund a new budget line?
3. Which pain is narrow enough for manual-first validation?
4. Which pain is not already becoming a built-in workflow from major platforms?

---

## Source notes

This document separates:

- source
- evidence
- inference
- recommendation

It does not treat hype about `AI agents` as proof of demand.

## Finding 1: There is strong interest in AI agents, but weak proof of scaled business value

### Source

- McKinsey, `The State of AI: Global Survey 2025`, published November 5, 2025
- McKinsey, `Seizing the agentic AI advantage`, published 2025
- Microsoft, `2025 Work Trend Index Annual Report`, published 2025

### Evidence

- McKinsey reports that almost all surveyed organizations are using AI, and `62%` are at least experimenting with AI agents.
- McKinsey also reports that nearly `two-thirds` of organizations have not begun scaling AI across the enterprise.
- McKinsey reports only `39%` say AI is impacting EBIT at the enterprise level.
- McKinsey says horizontal copilots and chatbots spread quickly, while more transformative vertical use cases are still mostly stuck in pilot mode.
- Microsoft reports `81%` of business leaders expect agents to be integrated into their AI strategy within the next 12 to 18 months.

### Inference

`AI Agent Development` is not suffering from lack of curiosity.
It is suffering from lack of trustworthy, provable, workflow-level value.

That means the business should not sell:

- "we build agents"
- "we build virtual employees"
- "we build multi-agent systems"

It should sell:

- one painful workflow
- one measurable operating result
- one low-risk first deployment surface

### Recommendation

Position around a business bottleneck, not around agent architecture.

## Finding 2: The market rewards vertical, workflow-specific outcomes more than broad assistant concepts

### Source

- McKinsey, `Seizing the agentic AI advantage`, published 2025
- Microsoft, `Leading the AI revolution: Insights from Microsoft's Work Trend Index`, published April 25, 2025

### Evidence

- McKinsey distinguishes between fast-spreading horizontal copilots and function-specific vertical use cases with deeper potential impact.
- Microsoft says `Sales, Operations, and Finance` are evolving into `agent-first` functions more than strategy-heavy or judgment-heavy domains.

### Inference

The strongest buyer demand is likely to appear where:

- work is repetitive
- work is expensive
- delay creates visible cost
- output can be checked by a human
- the workflow already exists before AI arrives

This supports workflow categories such as:

- sales follow-up
- customer support resolution
- finance/admin processing
- operations reporting and triage

This weakens broad positioning such as:

- AI executive assistant for everything
- multi-agent team for the whole business
- general-purpose internal helper

### Recommendation

If the business model is redesigned, it should start from one vertical workflow with measurable throughput or revenue effect.

## Finding 3: Buyers pay faster for revenue leakage and cost leakage than for abstract productivity

### Source

- HubSpot `Sales Trends Report`, surfaced in 2026 crawl, reporting that sales reps spend only `2 hours per day` actually selling
- AMP Bank Australia small business research, published March 17, 2025
- Zendesk 2025 CX Trends report press summary, published November 20, 2024

### Evidence

- HubSpot reports sales reps only spend around `2 hours per day` actually selling, and many believe AI can reduce manual work.
- AMP reports Australian small business owners are losing major time to admin, with `2 in 5` saying admin gets in the way of day-to-day running, and `1 in 5` saying they cannot keep up with financial administration.
- Zendesk reports strong demand for AI that helps agents resolve issues faster and reduce workload, with some organizations reporting large resolution-time gains.

### Inference

There are three broad pain classes with real economic weight:

1. `Revenue leakage`
   - missed follow-up
   - stalled proposals
   - poor qualification
   - slow response on warm opportunities

2. `Cost leakage`
   - repetitive admin
   - document handling
   - inbox and workflow triage
   - internal operational reporting

3. `Service backlog`
   - support workload
   - slow resolution
   - inconsistent triage

Revenue leakage is usually the easiest to sell early because the buyer already links the pain to money.
Cost leakage can also be strong, especially in Australia, but often needs clearer before/after math.
Service backlog is real, but increasingly crowded by vendor-native tooling.

### Recommendation

The first money path should likely start from either:

- revenue recovery
- admin-cost reduction with very clear time/value math

Not from vague time-saving language alone.

## Finding 4: Australian SMB and mid-market buyers already feel heavy admin and productivity pressure

### Source

- AMP Bank Australia small business research, published March 17, 2025
- MYOB Australia mid-sized business AI research, published October 7, 2025
- Xero Australia Small Business Insights, latest Australia release surfaced in 2026 crawl

### Evidence

- AMP reports strong time burden from admin among Australian small business owners.
- MYOB reports Australian mid-sized businesses are investing in AI to lift productivity, contain costs, and create operational efficiencies.
- Xero reporting points to continued productivity pressure and capacity constraints in Australian small business conditions.

### Inference

For Australian buyers, the problem frame should sound like:

- recover time lost to non-revenue work
- recover delayed revenue from neglected process
- avoid hiring before improving throughput
- tighten workflow without a full systems overhaul

This suggests Australia is likely a better fit for:

- workflow services with a clear ROI story
- short pilots
- human-in-the-loop delivery

It is likely a weaker fit for:

- abstract AI agent platform narratives
- complex multi-agent orchestration sold up front

### Recommendation

When we redesign the business model, the pitch should sound operational and commercial, not futuristic.

## Finding 5: The biggest market pain is not "building an agent"; it is moving from pilot to production safely

### Source

- IBM, `CIOs Face A Critical Gap As AI Risk Governance Falls Behind`, published October 22, 2025
- Deloitte, `Agentic AI is scaling faster than guardrails`, published 2025
- IBM, `AI Agent Governance: Big Challenges, Big Opportunities`, surfaced in 2026 crawl

### Evidence

- IBM reports nearly `74%` of surveyed organizations have only moderate or limited AI risk/governance coverage.
- Deloitte reports about `80%` of surveyed organizations lack mature agentic AI governance capabilities.
- IBM highlights challenges including agent opacity, decentralized deployment, audit difficulty, and inconsistent controls.

### Inference

This creates a second possible business model, different from "workflow implementation":

- not agent building
- but `agent deployment safety, controls, governance, monitoring, and human handoff design`

That is likely more enterprise-facing and less founder-SMB-facing.

So the business may actually split into two different lanes:

1. `SMB / mid-market workflow result lane`
   - sells a concrete workflow outcome
   - faster to validate
   - easier to pre-sell manually

2. `Enterprise enablement lane`
   - sells governance, controls, deployment design, observability
   - higher ticket, but longer cycle and stronger credibility requirements

### Recommendation

Do not mix these lanes in one first offer.
Pick one.

## Finding 6: Native platform absorption risk is real and rising

### Source

- Microsoft Dynamics 365 announcements on Sales Qualification Agent, Sales Close Agent, and role-based agents, published 2025
- Microsoft 365 and Work Trend reporting, published 2025
- Zendesk AI positioning, 2025-2026

### Evidence

- Microsoft is shipping built-in sales, finance, and service agents inside existing business software.
- Zendesk is shipping AI around support resolution and workflow orchestration.
- Major vendors are steadily bundling AI into systems of record.

### Inference

If the offer is:

- generic sales qualification
- generic support triage
- generic internal knowledge retrieval
- generic inbox assistant

then the business risks competing against platform bundling rather than selling a defensible service.

Safer value layers are:

- cross-tool workflow ownership
- implementation inside messy real customer operations
- human-in-the-loop review logic
- process redesign
- local or vertical-specific nuance
- change management and operational accountability

### Recommendation

The business model should emphasize the layer vendors do not fully own:

- applied workflow design
- messy implementation
- adoption
- monitoring
- business accountability for outcome

## Provisional pain map

### Strongest pain clusters for a first business model

1. `Revenue recovery workflows`
   - pain: missed follow-up, stale pipeline, dropped proposals
   - buyer logic: easy to connect to revenue
   - validation logic: can be done manually first
   - risk: some platform competition, but still defensible through execution

2. `Admin compression workflows`
   - pain: too much owner/admin time spent on repetitive tasks
   - buyer logic: strong in Australia if ROI is made concrete
   - validation logic: manual-first is easy
   - risk: can become a weak "save time" story unless quantified

3. `Service backlog workflows`
   - pain: high volume, slow response, inconsistent handling
   - buyer logic: strong in service-heavy orgs
   - validation logic: possible
   - risk: strong vendor bundling pressure

### Weaker starting positions

- generic AI chief of staff
- generic multi-agent team
- broad internal knowledge assistant
- "we build AI agents for any workflow"
- software platform before one painful workflow is proven

---

## Business model implications

### If the goal is fastest path to first money

Best shape:

- sell a bounded workflow result as a service
- manual or semi-manual delivery first
- price against revenue recovered or admin hours removed

### If the goal is bigger long-term defensibility

Best shape:

- begin with one workflow wedge
- build repeatable SOPs
- productize pieces later
- possibly expand into governance / monitoring / multi-workflow orchestration only after a real operational foothold exists

### If the goal is enterprise budgets

Best shape:

- sell safe deployment, governance, controls, observability, and human approval systems
- but this is a different GTM motion than founder-led SMB workflow services

## Recommendation

The current landing-page direction should not be treated as settled.

The real strategic question is not:

- "what should the page say?"

It is:

- "which pain lane do we want this business to monetise first?"

The strongest lanes to redesign around are:

1. `Revenue recovery`
2. `Admin compression`
3. `Enterprise agent governance`

My current recommendation is:

- keep `Revenue recovery` as the benchmark lane because it is the easiest to validate quickly
- but reopen the business model before finalizing the landing page, because `Admin compression` for Australian service businesses may also be a strong and more localizable angle
- treat `Enterprise agent governance` as a separate business, not just a feature add-on

## Next design questions

Before redoing the business model, answer:

1. Do we want to sell `recovered revenue`, `recovered time`, or `safe deployment` first?
2. Are we optimizing for `SMB / founder-led`, `mid-market operational teams`, or `enterprise IT / risk`?
3. Is the first offer a `done-for-you workflow service`, an `operated retainer`, or a `governed implementation project`?
4. Which pain lane gives us the strongest combination of:
   - willingness to pay
   - manual-first validation
   - defensibility against platform bundling
